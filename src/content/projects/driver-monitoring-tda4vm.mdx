## Overview

A driver monitoring system (DMS) watches the driver through a cabin camera and decides, frame by frame, whether they are focused, distracted, sleepy or drowsy. The EU General Safety Regulation now requires drowsiness and distraction warning in new cars, a cabin camera is the usual way to deliver it, and it has to run on the car's own hardware, not on a GPU in the trunk.

We built a complete DMS on a BeagleBone AI-64, a board based on TI's TDA4VM, an automotive vision SoC with Arm cores, C66x and C7x DSPs, a matrix-multiply accelerator and a vision pre-processing accelerator. The work is published at IEEE MIUCC 2024.

## How the system decides

Four cues describe the driver's state. **Eye closure** comes from the eye aspect ratio, computed from facial landmarks around each eye. **Yawning** is measured as the distance between the upper and lower lip landmarks. **Head pose** is estimated with OpenCV's `solvePnP`, matching a 3D head model to the 2D landmarks using the camera's calibration. **Gaze** comes from the pupil's position inside the eye region. A decision layer combines them into one of four states.

Faces are found with YOLOv5-ti-lite, TI's variant of YOLOv5. We started with YOLOv8, but TIDL, TI's deep-learning library and runtime, did not support some of its pooling layers and its SiLU activation. The model is exported to ONNX, compiled with the TIDL tools and quantised to 8-bit integers so it runs on the C7x DSP and its matrix-multiply accelerator. Landmarks come from Dlib's 68-point shape predictor, which is fast enough for an embedded target.

## Camera and pipeline on the SoC

The camera is an IR-sensitive IMX219 module on a two-lane MIPI CSI-2 link, so it keeps working at night and in changing light. I brought it up under embedded Linux with device-tree overlays and the capture drivers, so it shows up as a standard V4L2 device.

On top of it sits a GStreamer pipeline using TI's TIOVX-backed elements; TIOVX is TI's implementation of OpenVX. The point of the design is to keep each step on the hardware made for it:

- **Image processing and pre-processing** (debayering, denoising, scaling, colour conversion) on the vision pre-processing accelerator (VPAC) and a C66x DSP.
- **Face detection** on the C7x DSP and its matrix-multiply accelerator.
- **Post-processing** (head pose, eye aspect ratio, mouth distance) on the second C66x DSP.

## Making it real-time

The first deployment on the board was far too slow to warn anyone. I profiled every stage, from reading a frame through pre-processing, inference and post-processing to writing the output. The fixes came from removing work the system did not need:

- **Stop writing every frame to disk.** Stream the output over TCP from the GStreamer pipeline instead.
- **Process one face.** A DMS only cares about the driver.
- **Bypass frames that barely changed** since the previous one, using a difference threshold.

Together with the accelerator offload above, this made the system run in real time on the board.

## Testing without the board

To develop and test without hardware, I built a software-in-the-loop (SIL) environment on x86. It simulates the TDA4VM's cores with virtual CPUs, the imager with OpenCV, and the DSPs with parallel threads, so the same application runs in CI. We tested it with open-source datasets and with our own dataset recorded with the IMX219, which also carries the calibration that head-pose estimation needs.
